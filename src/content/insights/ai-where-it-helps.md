The question arrives in different forms. A principal reads something at a conference, or a peer mentions it in passing, or a technology platform sends another white paper. The question is always a version of the same thing: are we using AI?

The answer, in most offices, is yes. Tools are running. Something is happening with documents, or with portfolio reporting, or with how manager research gets pulled together. The AI adoption box has been ticked.

The most-cited figure in current family office research is that 86% of family offices use AI technology for operations and data. It is presented as evidence of progress - a sector moving quickly.

The less-cited figure is this: 65% of family offices still run core operations on spreadsheets.

These are not contradictory statistics. They describe the same offices. Most have added AI tools to processes that were not designed for them. That is adoption. It is not integration. The distinction matters because the risks are different - and the failure, when it comes, tends to be quiet.

AI works when the inputs are structured, the volume is high and the output does not require judgement. Three workflows in most family offices meet all three conditions.

Document processing is the clearest. Subscription documents, DDQ responses, regulatory filings, fund reports - these arrive in volume, follow predictable formats and consume significant staff time. Where offices have applied AI here, processing times have dropped from days to hours, sometimes to minutes. The time recovered goes not to headcount reduction but to the work that actually needed a human.

The second is reporting aggregation. Pulling data from multiple custodians, formats and asset classes into a coherent picture was a monthly exercise in manual reconciliation. Automated reporting adoption has moved from 46% to 69% across family offices in a single year. That is where the real time is being recovered.

The third is first-pass research synthesis: initial background on a manager, a sector scan, a summary of recent commentary. Useful as a starting point. Not the endpoint.

The risks are not in the workflows where AI is obviously unsuitable. They are in the ones where it is partially useful, and the part where it is not goes undetected.

Compliance-critical sign-offs are the most common example. When AI drafts a KYC memo and a human approves it, the audit trail says it was reviewed. Whether the reviewer interrogated it independently - or accepted it because the format looked right and the time pressure was real - is not recorded anywhere. The failure is invisible until it is not.

A related category is anything that depends on institutional memory: why a particular manager was retained after a difficult period, why a specific allocation has been held through volatility, what the family's actual risk appetite is in a liquidity event as opposed to what the policy document says. This knowledge lives in people, not in files. AI has no access to it. The outputs that depend on it will not say so.

The highest-stakes category is principal-facing reporting. A wrong figure in a quarterly summary does not just require a correction - it requires an explanation. And the explanation leads directly to a question about the reliability of the process that produced it.

> A tool bolted onto a broken process does not fix it. It only makes the failure quieter, and harder to see coming.

### The question that gets skipped

Most offices ask which tool to use, then identify workflows it might improve. The question that gets skipped is the one that comes before: what does this workflow need to look like before AI can make it better?

The answer involves three things. The inputs need to be clean and consistent. The outputs need to be auditable. And the process needs to already work reliably without AI - because automation accelerates what is already there, including the problems.

When those conditions are not in place, the failure is slow and quiet: a reporting discrepancy that takes three hours to trace, a compliance document approved because the format looked right, a principal question nobody can answer because the institutional knowledge was never written down.

The offices getting the most from AI are not the ones that have adopted the most tools.

They are the ones that started with the workflow: mapped what it actually required, tested whether the inputs were clean, confirmed the outputs could be audited and then asked whether AI improved it. Most of the time, it did. When it did not, they found out before it mattered.

The ones that skipped that step are still finding out.
