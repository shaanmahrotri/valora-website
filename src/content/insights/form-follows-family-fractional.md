Form follows function, the American architect Louis Sullivan insisted, and the dictum has outlived almost everything he built.

It applies rather well to the family office, an institution now quietly changing shape. For a generation the assumption was settled: serious wealth required a serious establishment - a chief executive, an investment team, in-house counsel, a brass plate in Mayfair. That model was never wrong, and for many families it remains exactly right. But it is no longer the only credible answer, and increasingly not the default one.

The office is starting to follow the family, rather than the family the office.

Several forces sit behind the shift, and none of them is cost alone. Family offices are getting leaner. Even substantial families have grown wary of the standing overhead, the key-person risk and the institutional inertia that a full permanent bench tends to accumulate. At the same time principals have become more mobile - living, working and holding interests across several jurisdictions - so an office anchored to a single city fits less neatly than it once did, and remote, distributed engagement is now ordinary.

Circumstances move faster, too. A liquidity event, a generational handover, a new operating business, a philanthropic turn: each changes what the family needs, and infrastructure that can flex to the moment is worth more than an establishment sized to the busiest of them. Technology has sharpened the point. Aggregation, reporting, origination and much of the oversight that once justified headcount now run on platforms, increasingly agentic ones, so the scarce resource is no longer hands but judgement - which tools to adopt, which to resist, and how to govern them safely.

None of this makes the full office obsolete. It makes it a choice rather than a reflex.

### The case for fractional

This is the space fractional engagement occupies. It is not a budget version of a full office; it is a different way to buy what a family office is actually for - design, governance and oversight - without the standing payroll that once came attached. The value was never in the bodies. It was in the architecture, in matching the office's form to the family's function. Done well it means working inside the office rather than over it: alongside the people already there, lending expertise rather than adding another voice to manage.

It also reaches families the traditional model never did - those below the scale a full office has always demanded, or whose wealth is still tied up in a business yet to be sold. Fractional engagement brings the same architecture within reach earlier, at a smaller base or before a liquidity event rather than only after it.

Several practical merits follow. The first is speed: assembling a fractional team typically takes much less time than a senior hire demands in search, notice and onboarding, and it can be unwound as easily. The second is fit for finite work - a sale or acquisition, a financing, a generational transition - the bounded projects that need real expertise for a season but cannot justify a permanent post once they are done. The third is currency of knowledge: working across several families at once, a fractional adviser brings to each the sharpened, continually refreshed perspective - including how peers are adopting AI safely - that no single in-house team, however able, can accumulate on its own.

What matters more than the office's size is the order in which it is built. The best are built from identity outward - values first, then a philosophy of governance, then the operations and investment policy that serve them. Reverse that order, as the industry often encourages, and you get an office organised around products and processes that no one can quite trace back to a purpose.

Foundation first. Operations second. Evolution always.

> An office built from identity outward can bend without breaking. One assembled around products has nothing to bend around.

Built in that order, an office holds together when it is tested - through a contested succession, a market dislocation, a dispute among those who inherit. And fractional engagement makes that sequence easier to honour, because it begins with the architecture rather than the staffing.

Independence is the most abused word in wealth management; almost everyone offering advice is also selling something - a fund, a platform, a product downstream - and most principals know it, even when the conflict arrives dressed as counsel. A fractional hire with no product to place and no balance sheet to feed can say the thing an incumbent will not: that the structure is wrong, that the manager is mediocre, or that the right move is to do less.

None of which makes fractional the answer for everyone. Some families genuinely warrant the full institution, where the scale of assets, the complexity of operating businesses or the sheer cadence of activity earns a permanent team its keep. And fractional adoption is still early: for all the recent talk, it remains a rising practice rather than an established norm, but the families adopting it now are ahead of the trend rather than following it.

What fractional offers the rest is the chance to build deliberately - an office shaped to the family's purpose, founded on who they are before what they own, and able to change as they do. Sullivan's rule holds: the shape should follow the need.

So the question worth asking, before the apparatus is commissioned, is not how large an office the family could build, but what it actually needs that office to do.
